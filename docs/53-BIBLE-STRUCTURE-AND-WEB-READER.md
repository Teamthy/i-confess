# Ledger 53 — The Bible structure, and the web Bible experience

**Date:** 2026-09-28
**Branch:** `arena/01a0e8b0-i-confess`
**Depends on:** `docs/51-BIBLE.md` (corpus, registry, verification), `docs/52-BIBLE-PLATFORM-ARCHITECTURE.md`
(provider boundary, rights model, migrations 0020–0023).
**Scope:** §5 *Bible structure* and §29 *Web Bible experience* of the platform brief, built on the
existing provider/rights/API layers. The canonical web app is `web/` — the original-design app PR #74
made canonical — and that is the surface this ledger delivers.

---

## 1. What was missing

The platform already had the hard parts: a `BibleProvider` boundary with a HelloAO adapter and a
reviewed local corpus, per-use rights on every translation, 40+ Bible endpoints, admin review, an
offline package builder and a Flutter reader. Two things were still absent, and they were the two a
reader actually touches first:

1. **The structure was not data anywhere a client could read it.** `internal/bible/canon.go` knew the
   66 books and their chapter counts, but nothing published them. Every Bible read was
   translation-scoped, so a client could not draw a book list, a chapter grid or a verse picker
   until a translation had loaded — and could draw nothing at all when the catalogue was empty. The
   section grouping a reader recognises (Law, History, Wisdom, the Prophets, the Gospels, the
   Letters) did not exist at all, and the alias table the reference parser resolves against was
   unexported, so a client-side parser had to guess at a second copy of it.

2. **`web/` had a 296-line Bible page.** A translation picker, a book dropdown, a chapter grid and a
   list of verses. No search, no verse actions, no highlights, bookmarks or notes, no comparison, no
   cross references, no plans, no reader settings, no deep links, no route for any of it, and no
   link back to the confessions that stand on the verse — which is the one thing that makes this a
   Bible *inside* iCONFESS.

## 2. The structure, as published data

`server/internal/bible/structure.go` computes the canon once and publishes it:

| Field | What it carries |
|---|---|
| `testaments[]` | Old and New, each with its sections, book count, chapter count and reference verse count |
| `sections[]` | `law`, `history`, `wisdom`, `major_prophets`, `minor_prophets`, `gospels`, `acts`, `pauline_letters`, `general_letters`, `apocalypse` — the ten blocks, each an ordered run of books |
| `books[]` | OSIS ID, USFM code, name, abbreviation, testament, section, canonical order, chapter count, verse count, **the reference verse count per chapter**, and every alias that opens it |
| `aliases` | The normalised alias table `ParseBook` itself resolves against — `john`, `jhn`, `1 cor`, `psalm`, `ii timothy` — published so a client-side parser cannot disagree with the server |

The section table is not a second hand-maintained list: it names the book that *opens* each section
and the builder walks `Canon` in order, so a section that drifts out of step with the canon fails a
test rather than mis-grouping a book.

Three consumers, one source:

- **`GET /v1/bible/structure`** — the canon, served from memory with `Cache-Control: public,
  max-age=86400`. No database, no provider, no translation required. With `?translation=` the same
  document is annotated with `available_book_ids` / `missing_book_ids` (and `extra_book_ids` for an
  edition that carries a book the Protestant canon does not), which is how a reader greys out
  Philippians in a New Testament that omits it instead of rendering an empty book.
- **`web/lib/canon.json`** — generated by `cd server && go run ./cmd/bible-structure
  ../web/lib/canon.json` (`make bible-structure`). 46 KB of structure and **no scripture text**.
- **`apps/mobile` / `clients/dart`** — unchanged this ledger, but the endpoint is the seam they take
  next.

`TestWebCanonJSONIsCurrent` in `internal/bible` fails when the checked-in JSON no longer matches the
Go table, so the copy in the web app cannot silently rot.

### Why the website may ship the structure but never the text

The shape of the canon is a fact about the canon: 66 books, 1,189 chapters, and a reference verse
distribution taken from the KJV. Verse *text* is a licensed asset whose redistribution, copying,
sharing, audio and indexing rights are reviewed per translation and enforced server-side. So the
structure is bundled and the text never is — which is also what makes the reader honest offline: it
can draw the whole Bible and say plainly that the words behind it are not reachable.

## 3. The web experience

Seventeen routes under `/bible`, all in `web/`:

```
/bible                                  home: continue reading, verse of the day, a reviewed
                                        random passage, reading stats, the whole canon as
                                        navigation, chapters the corpus cites
/bible/search                           phrase search and reference resolution, book filter
/bible/topics  /bible/topics/{slug}     the topical system, derived from the reviewed corpus
/bible/compare                          parallel reading, two to four translations
/bible/translations                     approved editions, per-use rights chips, licence, source
/bible/languages                        languages with an approved edition, RTL flagged
/bible/plans  /bible/plans/{slug}       reviewed reading plans and their days
/bible/highlights /bookmarks /notes
        /collections /history           the private library
/bible/settings                         typography, layout, reading palette, data export
/bible/audio                            rights-gated audio, signed URLs only
/bible/{translation}                    the reader in that edition
/bible/{translation}/{book}             the book, from chapter 1
/bible/{translation}/{book}/{chapter}   the canonical deep link
/bible/passage/{reference}              John.3.16, John/3/16 and "Psalm 23" all land on the verse
```

The reader (`web/components/bible-reader.tsx`):

- **Four modes** — standard, focus (chrome recedes, 66ch measure), study (a panel with the
  confessions in this chapter, your marks and the book's shape), audio.
- **Continuous reading** — next/prev cross book boundaries through the canon, ← → move chapters,
  swipe moves chapters on touch (mirrored under RTL), and the next chapter is prefetched as soon as
  the current one renders.
- **Verse actions** — seven highlight colours, bookmark, note, copy, share, listen, cross
  references, compare translations, add to collection, and *Confess from this verse* / *Reflect*,
  which carry the reference into the existing iCONFESS flows.
- **Rights obeyed, not re-decided** — copy, share and listen are disabled with a reason when the
  translation does not carry that grant; attribution is rendered whenever it is required.
- **Typography and palette** — size, line height, serif/sans, verse-per-line or flowing paragraphs,
  verse numbers on or off, and a reading palette (light, sepia, dark) that is independent of the
  site theme.
- **RTL** — direction comes from the translation's metadata, not the interface language.
- **Accessibility** — every verse is a real button with a label that announces its number and its
  marks, the reader is fully keyboard-operable, and the skeleton replaces a spinner.

The private library — highlights, bookmarks, notes, collections, history, chapter progress,
preferences — is keyed on the **canonical verse identity** (`JHN.3.16`), never a provider row ID, so
it survives a provider change. It is local to the browser under one key, and mirrored to
`/api/v1/me/bible/*` when a real session token is present; the page says which it is rather than
implying an account.

## 3a. Topics, random, and the parallel Bible

Three more pieces of the brief landed after the first pass, and all three are
built from content this platform has already reviewed rather than from a new
editorial surface nobody maintains.

**Topics (§41, §8).** `GET /v1/bible/topics` and `/v1/bible/topics/{slug}`
derive a topic from the published confession corpus: every canon-validated
citation a published confession makes, grouped by its published category, with
the passages ranked by how many confessions stand on them. Nothing is authored
twice, so adding a reviewed confession extends its topic automatically. Three
properties make it safe to serve: a citation that no longer parses against the
canon is **dropped, not guessed at**; a topic with no valid passage is absent
rather than published empty; and the endpoint returns *references only*, so a
topic page can never become a way around a translation's licence — the text is
read back through the normal rights-gated endpoints. `/bible/topics` and
`/bible/topics/{slug}` render it, and link across to the confessions in that
same area.

**Random (§8).** `GET /v1/bible/random` draws from that same reviewed citation
pool, resolves it in a rights-checked translation, and is `Cache-Control:
no-store`. Random is where a Bible app is most tempted to reach outside what it
has reviewed; this one cannot. Without a translation it asks for one (400)
rather than picking an edition on the reader's behalf.

**Parallel Bible (§13).** `/bible/compare` renders two to four approved
translations of one reference in responsive columns — one column on a phone,
up to four on a wide screen — each with its own direction and attribution. The
reference is the one thing every column shares.

**Analytics (§57).** `bible_opened`, `chapter_opened`, `verse_opened`,
`translation_selected`, `search_performed`, `highlight_created`,
`bookmark_created`, `note_created`, `topic_opened`, `bible_compare_opened`,
`reading_plan_started` and `bible_random_requested` go through the existing
`track()` seam. Note contents are never in an event — only that a note
happened.

## 3b. Contract drift found and fixed

Typing the client against the Go handlers surfaced four places where the web
app would have worked against the fixture and broken against production. All
four are now aligned in both the client and the fixture:

| Endpoint | Real contract | What the client had assumed |
|---|---|---|
| `verse-of-day` | `{date, editor_note, translation, verse, reference}` — a single verse, `reference` being the canonical ID (`PRO.3.5`) | a `passage` with a verse array |
| `compare` | `translations` is **required**, two to four names, else 400 | no `translations` parameter at all |
| `plans` / `plans/{slug}` | `duration_days`, `reading_count`; detail nested under `plan` with `days[].day_number` / `.references` | `day_count`, `days[].day`, un-nested |
| `audio` | `audio_url`, `alignment`, `expires_in_seconds` | `url` |

This is the argument for the fixture mirroring the handler rather than being
convenient: the fixture now enforces the same 2–4 rule and returns the same
field names, so the failure shows up locally instead of in production.

## 4. The link back to iCONFESS

`web/lib/bible.ts` indexes the reviewed confession corpus by canonical reference at load. That gives
three things no third-party Bible embed can give:

- a verse shows the confessions written from it, and links to them;
- the study panel lists the confessions in the chapter;
- the home page lists the chapters the corpus cites most, which is a measured fact about the corpus
  rather than an invented popularity metric.

## 5. Development fixture

Go cannot run in the sandbox the web preview runs in, so `scripts/dev-api.mjs` gained a Bible half
(`scripts/dev-bible.mjs`) serving the same shapes the Go handlers serve:

- **Structure** is the real generated canon — the same file the production API validates against.
- **Text** is real public-domain Scripture, fetched at build time by `scripts/dev-bible-corpus.py`
  (`make bible-corpus`) into `/tmp` and never committed: the **KJV** (aruljohn/Bible-kjv) and the
  **World English Bible** (TehShrike/world-english-bible). The converter audits both against the
  canon and prints the deviation — KJV **66 books / 1,189 chapters / 31,102 verses, +0** against the
  reference distribution; WEB **31,098, −4**.
- **Rights are modelled**: both editions carry `audio_allowed: false` because no recording has been
  registered or reviewed, and `GET /v1/bible/audio` answers 404 accordingly.
- **Cross references** are derived from the confession corpus — two passages are related because a
  reviewed confession stands on both.
- Without the corpus the fixture serves an empty catalogue and the reader shows its honest
  unavailable state, which is exactly what it must do against a real outage.

## 7. What is explicitly not done

- **Mobile and the Dart client were not touched.** The structure endpoint is the seam they should
  take; `apps/mobile` still builds its own book list. Flutter is unavailable in this sandbox.
- **`apps/web`** still has its own older `BibleReader`; only `web/` was rebuilt, per the scope
  chosen for this session.
- **Audio playback in the reader is device narration** (`speechSynthesis`), gated on the
  translation's audio grant, and clearly labelled as reading aloud on this device. Registered audio
  assets with verse alignment are served by `/v1/bible/audio` and surfaced on `/bible/audio`, but
  synchronised highlighting against a real recording is not built.
- **No live deployment, no managed database, no R2.** The preview runs against the local fixture.
- **Search is the API's** — the fixture scans its in-memory corpus, and production search remains
  the rights-scoped PostgreSQL index from `0023`. Semantic search is not built.
- **Semantic/AI search (§75)** is not built; cross references are corpus-derived in the fixture and
  admin-reviewed in production.
- Formal accessibility and RTL QA, and device testing, remain owed.
